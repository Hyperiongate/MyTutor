Character study · 28 August 2026

Mr. Cadabra, Sharpened

Your pencil idea, drawn out. A No.2 yellow wizard who balances on his own point, made of the same thing as the marks on his board, with a face drawn on rather than built in. Every line here is vector, so he holds at any size, in any expression, and costs nothing to show.

Concept · nothing shipped · no code touched
The hat carries the brandIt sits where the eraser used to be, and it is the one purple thing on him. The company stays purple; he goes yellow.
Drawn on, not built inEyes, brows and mouth are graphite strokes with a slight wobble — the same line quality as his handwriting on the board.
Arms are pencil linesThin graphite strokes with soft mitts — the only limbs he has. Nothing mechanical. He is a drawing that got up.
The ferrule is the tellThat silver band with two ridges is what still says “pencil” when he is 70 pixels tall and the tip is out of frame.
Six flats and a No.2 yellowFacet lines with a light and a dark side. The yellow does more work than any other decision here: nobody has to work out what he is.
He stands on his pointNo legs, no shoes, nothing to misread. A soft shadow underneath does the grounding, and leaning in to write is now one small tilt.

You were right

About that point

It read as a tail, and once you see it you cannot unsee it. The cause was mechanical: the tip sat at the same height as his shoes, so the eye grouped it with his legs instead of with the pencil. Three ways out, drawn rather than described.

Why B is the right call

Because a character with no legs has no anatomy to misread — not standing, not walking, not leaning, not at 44 pixels in a nav bar. You are not managing the problem any more, you have deleted it. And balancing on his own point is simply a better idea than standing next to it: it says the thing the whole character is for.

What you give up

Legs, and the walking they would have allowed. Honestly, not much: what he actually needs to do is arrive, stand, watch, lean in to write, and celebrate — and every one of those is better as a hop, a tilt or a bob than as a walk cycle. A hovering pencil is also easier to move around a screen than a walking one.

The bit that sells it

The soft shadow under his tip. Without it he looks like he is falling; with it he looks like he is balancing, and a wizard who balances on his own point reads as deliberate rather than unfinished. Costs one ellipse.

Why this works

Three things the pencil buys you that the robot never could

He is the subject

A robot is a tutor-shaped thing borrowed from science fiction. A pencil is what the child is holding. The mascot and the work are finally the same material, and every visual joke you will ever want — writing, erasing, getting shorter, being sharpened — comes free with the shape.

Nobody mistakes him for Abrabot

Today two robots share one screen and you told me they were indistinguishable. Tall and pointed beside short and boxy is a difference a child reads in bad lighting, at a glance, without knowing why. That is a silhouette problem solved rather than a colour problem managed.

He can never fall in the valley

Your standing rule is that clearly-a-character beats almost-lifelike, especially for children. A pencil is the most committed version of that rule you can make. It also means his face can be animated cheaply, forever, with no render bill.

The mouth

He talks

Press it. The mouth opens and closes on the loud parts and rests closed on the quiet ones, the brows lift a little when he emphasises, and he blinks on his own every few seconds — that last one costs nothing and is most of what makes a face look alive.

How it should be wired, and this is the good part. Not a random loop. The browser can read the actual loudness of the clip that is playing, sixty times a second, straight off the audio element. The mouth follows the real voice. That means it works with the ElevenLabs lines you have already paid for — every one of them, including all six thousand of Prealgebra — with no re-rendering, no new audio, and no per-word work by anyone.

And on the voice itself: keep it. A warm adult voice coming out of a clearly-drawn character is how nearly every animated teacher has ever worked, and the mismatch people actually notice runs the other way — a cartoon voice would make him a toy. The one thing to watch is that he is drawn as a grown-up: the hat, the calm brows, the unhurried blink. Get that right and the voice you bought fits him better than it fits the robot.

The size that actually matters

In the orb, at real pixels

These are not scaled illustrations — they are the working sizes, on the session page’s dark circle. This is where a mascot usually dies, so it is the first place to look.

The honest problem

A pencil is tall and thin and a face slot is round, so upright he leaves air on both sides and reads small. The robot filled that circle because a robot head is round.

The fix, and I like it better anyway

Two changes. A shorter hat for the small slot, and a lean of about eighteen degrees — the diagonal is the longest line in a circle, so he grows by a third without getting bigger. And the lean earns its keep: he now reads as leaning in to look at the child’s work. A posture, not a workaround.

The build mn test

Tell them apart at seventy pixels

The robot on the right stands in for Abrabot — his real design is the one already in the app. Yellow against Abrabot’s cyan now separates them on colour as well as shape, which is two independent reasons a child can tell them apart instead of one.

Range

Five faces off one body

Same character, five states, no new artwork — only the strokes on his barrel change. This is the part that would be impossible with video and expensive with 3D.

The moment worth having

He writes with himself

He does not hold a pen. He leans in, kicks his legs back, and his own point leaves the line. Note what that buys us: standing, his taper is blunt and tucked away — the sharp point only appears in the pose where it means something. That is the cleanest possible answer to your worry. The point is not a permanent feature of his body. It is something he does.

Before you say yes

What this costs you

The video presence work

This is the real bill. The presence layer puts a lifelike human face in the same circular slot, and a pencil and a photorealistic person cannot be the same character. Choosing the pencil shelves that path, or turns those clips into a narrator who is explicitly not him.

Worth saying plainly: your own vision doc already sequences an animated character before any photoreal attempt. The pencil is not a detour from that plan. It is that plan, with a shape.

The pixel pins

Six pages render his face and read the pixels back to prove he is still purple. Going yellow means every one of those gets repointed — mechanical work, but be clear that it is a brand decision and not a side effect of the drawing.

The version I would defend: build u already separated the two, the robot being him and the logo being the company. So the company stays purple — the site, the logo, the accent — and yellow becomes his, with the purple riding on his hat so the two are visibly related. He gains more than he gives up: on the dark orb yellow is brighter than the purple ever was, and nobody has to work out what he is.

The magic problem

Your promise is that he never just gives the answer. A wizard who waves a wand and produces one contradicts that in a single image. So: the hat is a hat. He owns no wand, nothing appears in a puff, and the only trick he ever performs is that the child suddenly understands. That line is worth writing down before an illustrator ever draws him holding something.

What I do not have

tutor-face.js itself. I still have not read it, so I do not know how many pins name a colour, whether the palette is reachable from outside, or what the presence layer does to the canvas parent. The yellow here is my own #F2BC1B — a standard No.2 — and the purple on his hat is #6B44BE, which should be replaced with the real brand purple out of the file. Send it and the estimate stops being an estimate.

Yours to answer

Four decisions

  1. Confirm B and the yellowBoth are drawn above and used everywhere on this page. If they hold up at full size on your own screen, the character is settled and the rest is engineering.
  2. Does he wear out?A pencil that got shorter over a school year is a lovely idea and a support nightmare. I would say no — he is the same height forever.
  3. Hat on always?Including in the small orb, where it costs vertical room. I say yes: at 70 pixels the hat is half of what makes him him.
  4. Does the video presence path close?The one answer I cannot give you. Everything else follows from it.
